# omelettehedron 🥚

**Live demo: https://omelettehedron.arhan.sh/**

omelettehedron is a fast solver for the face-turning octahedron (FTO): a two-phase solver that
finds short solutions within milliseconds and can prove them optimal. **This project was entirely
generated by AI** and was made possible under a few special conditions:

- I recently attended YCombinator's [startup school](https://www.startupschool.org/) and was gifted 6 months of Anthropic's Claude Ultra plan;
- as well $10,000 in Microsoft Azure credits
- I have had an interest in computational Rubik's Cube theory

I created a 48 core AMD EPYC 9V45 virtual machine with 94 GiB and three 300GB data disks. I gave
Claude Code's Opus 5.5 model full access over the virtual machine and asked it to autonomously
create the solver.

## Overview

A fast two-phase solver for the face-turning octahedron (FTO), written in Rust, and a separate
[optimal solver](#optimal-solver) (single-phase IDA*).

* **Phase 1** brings the puzzle into H_A, the positions that can be solved by turning
  only the four class-A faces U, F, BR and BL.
* **Phase 2** finishes with those faces only. Its 933,120 positions are fully tabulated, so it
  is an O(length) lookup, and its solutions are optimal even among all face-turn solutions.

Phase 1 is an IDA* search guided by pruning tables. See [docs/PHASES.md](docs/PHASES.md)
for why this split was chosen and how it compares with alternatives (and
[docs/CHEN.md](docs/CHEN.md) for Chen Shuang's block split, which we measured and set aside),
[docs/RESULTS.md](docs/RESULTS.md) for measured speed and solution lengths, and
[docs/OPTIMIZATION_LOG.md](docs/OPTIMIZATION_LOG.md) for how it got there.

On 48 threads a random position gets its first solution in about 6 ms (23 moves on average),
21.5 moves after 1 s and 21.2 after 5 s; a proof of optimality takes from a few seconds to about
a minute (optimal lengths are around 20-21).

## Build

```sh
cargo build --release
```

On x86-64 the build targets `x86-64-v3` (AVX2/BMI2, any x86-64 CPU from about 2015 on); see
`.cargo/config.toml`. Other targets build too: aarch64 (e.g. Apple Silicon) uses NEON for
the byte shuffles, wasm32 uses simd128 when enabled (`-C target-feature=+simd128`), and
anything else falls back to scalar code.

## Notation

Hold the puzzle with a corner pointing at you:

| Side | Faces |
|---|---|
| Front | U (top), R (right), F (bottom), L (left) |
| Back | B (top), BR, D (bottom), BL |

`X` turns face X 120° clockwise as seen from outside, and `X'` turns it anticlockwise. `X2` is
accepted as two turns. Opposite pairs: U–D, F–B, R–BL, L–BR.

`Xw` turns face X together with the middle layer (a wide turn). `Xs` turns only the middle
layer between X and its opposite face, in the sense of X, so `Us` = `Ds'` (a slice turn).
Scrambles may always contain wide and slice turns; solutions name slices by their front face
(`Us`, `Rs`, `Fs`, `Ls`). `Xv` in a scramble turns the whole puzzle like X (all three layers of
X's axis).

`solve ... -v` also says whether the solution solves the *pure* FTO, where every centre triangle is
unique: the solver itself treats centres of a colour as interchangeable, so its optimum is a
lower bound for the pure puzzle. `bench/hard.tsv` lists hard positions (`scripts/bench-hard.sh`).

Face turns can rotate the whole puzzle, so a solution ends in the solved state in *some*
orientation. Internally the front corner is held fixed.

### Move sets

Solutions use face turns, plus the families given with `--moves` (comma-separated). Every
allowed move counts as one.

| `--moves` | Solutions use | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| (none) | face turns | the face-turn metric |
| `slice` | face and slice turns | shorter solutions; own pruning tables (`*-slice.prun`) |
| `wide` | face and wide turns | same lengths and search; see below |
| `wide,slice` | all three | as `slice`, printed as with `wide` |

A wide turn is a turn of the opposite face plus a whole-puzzle rotation, and the solver treats
every orientation as solved, so wide turns never shorten a solution. With `wide` the search is
unchanged and only the output differs: the corner piece between U, R, F and L stays where the
scramble left it, and turns of the faces around it are printed as wide turns of the opposite
faces (`Dw` for `U` if the scramble left that piece in front).

With `slice` the phase-1 search has 24 internal moves instead of 16, and slices are not
phase-2 moves; see [docs/PHASES.md](docs/PHASES.md#8-move-sets---moves) for the move-order
rules, the tables and why optimal mode stays exact.

## Usage

| Command | What it does |
|---|---|
| `solve two-phase` | the two-phase solver: short solutions within milliseconds, then better ones until the best is proven optimal |
| `solve optimal` | the [optimal solver](#optimal-solver): its first solution is optimal |
| `solve combined` | [both at once](#both-solvers-at-once), until their bounds meet |
| `gen two-phase`, `gen optimal` | generate (or check) a solver's [pruning tables](#pruning-tables) ahead of time |
| `bench two-phase`, `bench optimal` | [benchmark](#benchmarks) a solver on seeded positions |
| `show` | describe a [position](#show-a-position) (moves or [pattern](#patterns)) without any table |
| `symmetric` | list the [positions that symmetries fix](#symmetric-positions) |

`omelettehedron help <command>` (or `--help`) lists every option.

```sh
# each better solution as it is found, until the best is proven optimal (Ctrl-C stops it)
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large "U R' BL F D B' L BR"

# stop after 2 seconds
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large -t 2 "R U' L F' BR B' ..."

# prove optimality faster, with target-length passes (about 2.6x faster on one thread), but
# no solution is printed until the optimal one
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large --passes "U R' BL F D B' L BR"

# stop at the first solution of at most 22 moves
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large -l 22 "..."

# stop after 1000 solutions (every phase-2 completion counts, even if longer than the best)
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large -n 1000 "..."

# print only the final solution (one line per position) and nothing else
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large -t 1 -q "..."

# one scramble per line on stdin
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large -t 1 < scrambles.txt

# a position given as the algorithm that solves it: solve that algorithm's inverse
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large --inverse "U F BL B' U B' BL BR' ..."

# show what the search is doing while it runs (stderr; see "Progress" below)
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large --progress "U R' BL F D B' L BR"

# resume a long optimal proof: an earlier run (--passes --progress) printed "pass 20 done: no
# solution of at most 20 moves", so no solution is shorter than 21 (a wrong bound can give a
# wrong answer)
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large --passes --progress --lower-bound 21 "..."

# slice turns allowed (generates the slice tables on first use)
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large --moves slice "U R' BL F D B' L BR"

# without --tier: the largest tier whose tables are all there (see Pruning tables)
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase "U R' BL F D B' L BR"
```

The three `solve` methods take the same options for the positions (a scramble, a
[pattern](#patterns), one per line on stdin, `--patterns`, `--random N --seed S`, `--inverse`),
the limits (`-t`, `-l`), the tables (`--tables`, `--moves`, `--force`, `--min-free-memory`) and
the output (`-q`, `-v`, `--progress`), and print the same way. On stdout, each new bound comes
as soon as it is found, with the time and the solver: every better solution (`upper`, the
optimal length is at most this) and every larger proven lower bound (`lower`, no solution has
fewer moves). Then come the solution and its length, and how far it is proven:

```text
$ omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large "U R' BL F D B' L BR"
loaded tables/ae-sym288-chiral.prun (1031.9 MB)
loaded tables/cb-sym24.prun (4.7 MB)
loaded tables/cbe.prun (6992.8 MB)
found tables/cbe-solved11.prun (read when optimal mode can use it)
    0.000 s  lower  6  two-phase
    0.000 s  upper  8  two-phase  BR' L' B D' F' R BL' U'
    0.000 s  lower  8  two-phase
BR' L' B D' F' R BL' U'
8 moves, optimal (solution: two-phase at 0.000 s; lower bound: two-phase at 0.000 s); 0.000 s
```

On stderr come the tier chosen (without `--tier`) and the tables as they are loaded (or
generated); `-v` adds the solver's statistics and whether the solution also solves the pure
puzzle (see [Notation](#notation)). With several positions (or `-v`), each one's output starts
with `# <position>`. `-q` prints only the solution (or `no solution found`), one line per
position, and nothing on stderr but errors.

Limits can be combined; the search stops when any of them triggers. `-j N` sets the thread
count (default: all cores). `--batch` (`solve two-phase`, `solve optimal`) solves many positions
at once, one per thread, and prints each one's output in input order.

### Show a position

`show` describes a position without loading any table (it takes well under a second). It takes
scrambles and patterns like `solve` (arguments or stdin, `--inverse`, `--patterns`, `--random
N`); `--json` prints one line of JSON per position.

```sh
target/release/omelettehedron show "U R' BL F D B' L BR"
# moves: U R' BL F D B' L BR (8 moves)
# pattern: GAOOAOWAW BBBBRRPPP GOOAGGGWO RRYYYRBBB / RRYYPRRBB OOGGWAGAO PPPPYYYPY AAWWWWWGA
# solved: no
# symmetries: none (only the identity)
# symmetry group: C1, subgroup 33 in `omelettehedron symmetric` (E)
# pieces (with the puzzle held so that most stickers are right):
#   corners: 1 of 6 solved
#   edges: 4 of 12 solved
#   centres: 13 of 24 on a face of their colour (6 of 12 on U F BR BL, 7 of 12 on R L B D)
#   pure FTO (every centre unique): 10 of the 13 are their own piece, 3 exchanged with a centre of the same colour
# in H_A (solvable with U F BR BL alone): no
# in H_B (solvable with R L B D alone): no
# lower bound: 7 moves (the corners alone need 3, the centres 7 and 4, the edges 7)
```

* Moves (for a scramble): the scramble as given and how many turns it has (a whole-puzzle
  rotation `Xv` counts none).
* Pattern: the 72 sticker colours the position shows (see [Patterns](#patterns)).
* Symmetries: how many of the octahedron's 48 symmetries (turning or mirroring the whole
  puzzle, colours renamed to match) give the same position. The optimal solver uses them. The
  symmetry group is the one of that number in the table of `symmetric`, with its kinds of
  symmetry (see [Symmetric positions](#symmetric-positions)).
* Pieces are counted with the puzzle held the way (of the 12 that turns can reach) that puts
  the most stickers on a face of their colour.
* Pure FTO: whether centres of a colour are exchanged. Only a scramble tells which centre is
  which, so a pattern or a random position says "not known".
* `H_A` and `H_B` are the two-phase solver's target groups (see [docs/PHASES.md](docs/PHASES.md));
  for a position in one, the exact count of turns of its four faces is given.
* The lower bound (face turns) is the largest distance to solved of the corners, the centres of
  each half and the edges' places, each taken alone (small tables built in memory).

### Symmetric positions

`symmetric` counts and lists the positions that symmetries of the octahedron fix (turning or
mirroring the whole puzzle, colours renamed to match: the symmetries `show` counts). It needs no
table and takes a few seconds.

```text
$ omelettehedron symmetric
 # order name conj                    fixed                  exactly           up to symmetry  symmetries
 1    48   Oh    1                        8                        8                        8  E 8C3 3C2 6m' 6S4 6C2' 6C4 i 8S6 3m
 2    24   Th    1                       32                       24                       12  E 8C3 3C2 i 8S6 3m
 ...
32     2   C2    3             603979776000             603959367680              75494920960  E C2
33     1   C1    1  31408133379194880000000  31408133376309204485184    654336112006441760108  E
```

* The symmetries that fix a position form a group. Up to turning and mirroring there are 33
  such groups (`name`, `conj`: how many copies of it the 48 symmetries contain).
* `fixed`: the positions the group fixes. `exactly`: those it fixes and no other symmetry does.
  `up to symmetry`: the same, counting once the positions that are turned or mirrored copies
  of each other (they are the same distance from solved).
* The kinds of symmetry: `C3` a third of a turn about a face axis; `C4`, `C2` a quarter and a
  half turn about a corner axis; `C2'` a half turn about an edge axis; `i` the point
  reflection; `m` a mirror through four corners, `m'` through two; `S4`, `S6` a turn and a
  mirror.

`--order N` or `--subgroup K` lists the positions, one of each set of copies, as patterns
(one per line, with a `#` comment) that `solve` and `show` read from stdin. `--batch` solves many
positions at once, one per thread:

```sh
omelettehedron symmetric --order 24 | omelettehedron solve two-phase --batch -l 22 -t 3
```

`symmetric --identify` says where given positions are listed (e.g. `position 2.4`).

The distances of the symmetric positions are in [docs/SYMMETRIC.md](docs/SYMMETRIC.md).

### Progress

`--progress` (for every `solve` method) prints what the search is doing to stderr while it
runs: each iteration as it ends (its depth, nodes, nodes per second, the best length so far and
the lower bound proven so far), each target-length pass that ends ("no solution of at most T
moves", with `--passes`), and, during a long iteration, a line every second with the nodes so
far and how many of its tasks have started. `--progress=5` prints that line every 5 seconds,
`--progress=0` never. The search does no extra work for it: on 48 threads it is as
fast as without, and short solves on one thread (24 ms each) take about 0.4 ms longer, the
time to print their 50 or so lines ([docs/RESULTS.md](docs/RESULTS.md#progress-and-both-solvers-at-once)).
Without `--progress` the output is unchanged.

solution length searched, and a completed depth proves that no solution has at most D moves.

### Patterns

A position can also be given as what it looks like: its 72 sticker colours (a *pattern*), for
example to find a scramble for a scrambled puzzle whose scramble is not known.

```sh
# the pattern a scramble makes (and more: see "Show a position")
target/release/omelettehedron show "R U' BL F D B' R L' BR U F' D"
# moves: R U' BL F D B' R L' BR U F' D (12 moves)
# pattern: OOGAAGGOG BYRPBYRPR AWAWGOGWA RPBBPRPPY / RBYYYPRBB GOWWWAGGW PYYRYRBBP OAAOWOAWO
# ...

# solve a pattern (given where a scramble would be); --usual gives your solved puzzle as you
# usually hold it (the colours of U R F L B BL D BR), for a scramble from that hold
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large -t 5 --usual WRGPBOYA \
    "OOGAAGGOG BYRPBYRPR AWAWGOGWA RPBBPRPPY / RBYYYPRBB GOWWWAGGW PYYRYRBBP OAAOWOAWO"
#     0.000 s  lower 10  two-phase
#     0.000 s  lower 12  two-phase
#     0.000 s  upper 12  two-phase  D' F U' L BR' R' B D' F' BL' U R'
# D' F U' L BR' R' B D' F' BL' U R'
# 12 moves, optimal (solution: two-phase at 0.000 s; lower bound: two-phase at 0.000 s); 0.000 s
# scramble from solved with W on U and G on F: R U' BL F D B' R BR L' U F' D
# scramble from your usual hold: R U' BL F D B' R BR L' U F' D

# several patterns from a file (or stdin), each solved as a single one
target/release/omelettehedron solve two-phase --tier large -t 5 --usual WRGPBOYA --patterns < patterns.txt
```

A position given as text (an argument, or a line of stdin) is read as a scramble if it is one,
else as a pattern. With `--patterns`, `solve` and `show` read patterns from stdin and number
them in the output (`# pattern 2 (line 4): ...`). A pattern can take one line or several: lines
are joined until they hold 72 stickers. A blank line ends a pattern early, so a pattern with a
sticker missing does not run into the next one. `#` starts a comment. A pattern with a mistake
gets its error message, the others are still solved, and the run then exits with an error.
Every `solve` method takes patterns and `--usual`.

Hold the puzzle with a corner pointing at you and read the front faces U, R, F, L (top, right,
bottom, left), then turn the whole puzzle around, keeping the top at the top, and read the back
faces B, BL, D, BR (top, right, bottom, left). Read each face with its outer edge at the top: the
5 stickers along that edge from left to right, the 3 of the middle row, then the one at the
corner in the middle. Spaces and `/` are ignored. Any 8 letters work, the puzzle may be held any
way and its colour scheme does not matter: which colour belongs to which face is worked out from
the corners. A pattern with a mistake gets a plain explanation (a colour on the wrong number of
stickers, a piece that cannot exist, two identical pieces, or a pattern that turning cannot make,
such as two edges swapped).

`show` prints the standard colour scheme (that of the Diansheng, Dayan and most other FTOs)
held the standard way, white on U and green on F, the scrambling orientation of FTO competitions:
W R G P / B O Y A = white, red, green, purple / blue, orange, yellow, grey on U R F L / B BL D BR.
Opposite faces are white/yellow, green/blue, red/orange and purple/grey (checked on a physical
puzzle, which also settles which of red and purple is on R). LanLan FTOs have red and green
swapped (so white on U and green on F is not possible on them).

The FTO has no fixed centres, so the same scrambled puzzle can be held 24 ways, and a scramble
(the solution reversed) makes the pattern exactly only from the solved puzzle held the way the
solution ends; the output says which (the colours on U and F). With `--usual`, the scramble
starts from your usual hold: it begins with up to two whole-puzzle rotations (`v` turns, such as
`Lv`) when the pattern was entered holding the puzzle one of the 12 ways rotations reach; for the
other 12 (the colours of the two halves of the faces swapped, a quarter turn about a corner) no
scramble from the usual hold makes the pattern as entered, and the output gives the one that
makes the same puzzle, held another way.

### Pruning tables

Choose the largest tier that fits comfortably in RAM. Tables are generated on first use. They
are written to `./tables`, or to `--tables DIR` or `$FTO_TABLES` if given.
`omelettehedron gen two-phase --tier T` generates them ahead of time (`gen optimal --tier T`
those of the [optimal solver](#optimal-solver)). Before
generating a table, the solver checks that the disk and the free memory can hold it, and stops
at once with a message if not.

Without `--tier`, `solve` and `bench` use the largest tier whose tables are all in the table
directory, and say which on stderr. A file counts if its size and header are right; the tables
are not read to decide. With `--moves slice` the `-slice` files count. If no tier is complete,
the command stops and says to pass `--tier` or to run `gen two-phase --tier T`; nothing is
generated without `--tier`. The optimal solver chooses among its own tiers the same way.

| `--tier` | Memory | Generation (48 cores) | Contents |
|---|---|---|---|
| `tiny` | ~42 MB | ~0.3 s | two small A-centre/edge tables read through relabellings (for low-memory devices; see below) |
| `small` | ~135 MB | ~1 s | A-centre/edge table without edge chirality |
| `medium` | ~1.0 GB | ~7 s | full A-centre/edge table (288-fold symmetry) |
| `large` | ~11.6 GB | ~50 s | medium + joint corner/B-centre/edge-triple table (about 2x fewer nodes) + a 3.5 GB distance-to-solved table on the same index (proofs, with or without a time limit: about 0.8x the time) |
| `huge` | ~34 GB | ~3.5 min | relabel-only A-centre/edge table (cheaper lookups) + the joint and distance-to-solved tables |

With `FTO_HUGE1G=1`, tables of 256 MB or more are placed on 1 GiB pages when the system has
reserved them (`echo N > /sys/kernel/mm/hugepages/hugepages-1048576kB/nr_hugepages` as root; the
large tier needs 13): optimal proofs about 4% faster with the A-centre/edge table on a 1 GiB page.
Without reserved pages the tables use transparent huge pages.

All tiers also use the 4.7 MB corner/B-centre table and the in-memory phase-2 table. With
`--moves slice` every tier has its own set of tables of the same sizes (names ending in
`-slice`), except the distance-to-solved table, which is for face turns only; `gen two-phase
--tier T --moves slice` generates them ahead of time. The distance-to-solved table is read only
when a solve gets deep enough to use it (on a background thread, while the search goes on), so
short solves and proofs start as fast as without it; long time-limited solves use it too
(corner 6-flip under `-t 1800`: 624 -> 84 s).
Generation needs about the memory of the tier, plus 7 GB while the distance-to-solved table is
made (peak 18.3 GB for `large`; `huge` 29.4 GB without that table, not measured with it); on
one core it takes ~5 s for `tiny`, ~30 s for `small` and ~3 min for `medium`. Writing the 30 GB
of `huge` to disk is a large part of its time.

The `tiny` tier (`src/light.rs`; the demo's Light) replaces the small tier's 129 MB
A-centre/edge table by two smaller ones: the A-centres x the places of the edges of two of the
four A-triples (36.3 MB, 145M entries under 48 symmetries), read for all six pairs of triples
through relabellings, and the A-centres x which edges belong to which half of a split of the
triples into two pairs (0.9 MB), read where the first says "at most 7". The bound is the largest
of these lookups, so the entries are not distances mod 3 but saturating codes (the first: at most
7, 8, 9, 10 or more; the second: at most 4, 5, 6, 7 or more), and the search reads them in
stages: the first lookup's word is prefetched with the others of its stage, and the rest only
for children the first does not cut. Estimated from 4M random positions it needs 1.5x the nodes of
the small tier (measured: 1.9x on the demo test's 14-move scramble, about 2.8x on 15-move
scrambles), and a node costs about twice as much: on one thread it finds a first solution for
about half as many random positions within 5 s.

## Benchmarks

Comparisons use the quick set, which takes about a minute per build and round on 48 threads (two
builds in two interleaved rounds stay under 5 minutes): the two-phase solver's optimal mode on
ten 17-move scrambles (1 thread) and ten 19-move scrambles (all threads), its time-limited mode
on ten random positions for 1 s each (all threads and 1 thread), and the optimal solver on the
same two optimal sets. Single items vary by up to about 5% between rounds (a comparison that
left the optimal solver unchanged gave 0.976x and 1.018x on its items), as large as most
per-node gains. So a change that claims less than about 5% runs at least 3 rounds of only the
items it touches (`--items`, still under 5 minutes) and counts as a gain only if it is better in
every round of every one of them (the "B better" column); 1-thread node counts repeat exactly
and decide for changes to the pruning. The longer suites below are for occasional runs.

```sh
scripts/bench-quick.sh <label>                  # build this tree, one round of the quick set
scripts/bench-quick.sh --compare <binA> <binB>  # two builds, interleaved rounds, markdown table
scripts/bench-quick.sh --rounds 4 --items 2p-t1s-mt,2p-t1s-1t --compare <binA> <binB>  # small gains
scripts/bench-quick.sh --opt-tier medium,large --rounds 2 <label>   # more tiers and rounds
target/release/omelettehedron bench two-phase --tier large -N 40 -t 5       # 40 seeded random positions, 5 s each
target/release/omelettehedron bench two-phase --tier large -N 20 -t 5 -j 1  # single-threaded
scripts/bench.sh <label> <tier>                 # the long standard set (about 5 minutes)
scripts/bench-opt.sh <label> <tier>             # optimal mode: time to prove optimality
scripts/bench-optimal.sh <label> <tier>         # the optimal solver: 17-move (1 thread), 19-move sets
scripts/bench-randopt.sh <label> <tier>         # optimal mode on random positions (slow)
scripts/compare.py <before> <after>             # markdown comparison of two runs
scripts/compare-optimal.py <before> <after>     # the same for scripts/bench-optimal.sh runs
```

`bench` reports these figures:
* mean best length after 0.01 s, 0.1 s, 1 s, … and at the time limit;
* mean time to the first solution and its length;
* how many positions were proven optimal;
* search speed in nodes per second.

## Optimal solver

`omelettehedron solve optimal` is a second, separate solver: a single-phase IDA* straight to the
solved state with pattern databases of distances to solved (as in optimal Rubik's Cube
solvers), in `src/optimal/`. It has its own tiers and tables (`opt-*.prun`) and its own
benchmark; see [docs/OPTIMAL.md](docs/OPTIMAL.md). The two-phase solver's optimal mode is
still faster on most positions (1.7x to 3.5x on 19- to 21-move positions against the large
tier, more where it finds the optimum early), but the optimal solver's large tier proves the
corner 6-flip in half a second, against ten minutes for the two-phase solver. For the hardest
positions (deep, symmetric, or with the edges and centres nearly solved), `solve optimal --tier
large` can prove optimality much faster than the two-phase solver: it proves the 23-move
tri_adj+edge_opp in 4.5 minutes, against 2.5 hours, and all four 23-move positions of
`bench/hard.tsv` in 45 minutes, against 11 hours.

```sh
target/release/omelettehedron solve optimal --tier medium "U R' BL F D B' L BR"
target/release/omelettehedron solve optimal --tier medium -n 10 -v "..."   # up to 10 optimal solutions
target/release/omelettehedron bench optimal --tier medium -N 10 --scramble-len 17 --seed 1717 -j 1
target/release/omelettehedron solve optimal --tier medium --moves slice "..."  # slice turns allowed
```

| `solve optimal --tier` | Memory | Tables |
|---|---|---|
| `small` | 195 MB | corners x centres (both orbits, 24 symmetries), edge permutation, and the two-phase A-centre/edge-triple table on both sides |
| `medium` | 3.4 GB | corners x centres, corners x edges (48 symmetries), and the two-phase A-centre/edge table on both sides |
| `large` | 22.0 GB | the two-phase A-centre/edge table on both sides and corners x B-centres x the edges of one face, to solved (21 GB, 6 symmetries, read for all 8 faces); `gen optimal --tier large` takes about 2 minutes on 48 cores (`--min-free-memory GB` stops it cleanly if memory runs low) |

Without `--tier`, `solve optimal` and `bench optimal` use the largest tier whose tables are all
there (see [Pruning tables](#pruning-tables)); `gen optimal` needs `--tier`.

It prints as the two-phase solver does: each lower bound as it is proven (every completed
depth), then the solution and the result line. Limits: none (first optimal solution), `-n N`
(up to N optimal solutions), `-t T` (time limit; prints the lower bound proven so far), `-l L`
(give up above L moves). Positions
that some of the octahedron's 48 symmetries fix (like most of `bench/hard.tsv`) are searched
with a symmetric start (only one of each set of equivalent first moves is searched), which
divides their node counts by about the number of symmetries.

`--start-depth D` skips the iterations below D: one iteration at D - 2 (cheap; it orders the
next one's tasks), then D. They find any solution of at most their depth, shorter ones too,
but a solution they find is not proven optimal. With `-l D` it answers whether a position needs more than D moves
without the iterations below D: `--start-depth 23 -l 23` either finds a solution of at most 23
moves or prints "no solution of at most 23 moves".

## Both solvers at once

`omelettehedron solve combined` runs the two-phase solver and the optimal solver on the same
position at the same time, each on half of the threads. The two-phase solver finds short
solutions fast: upper bounds on the optimal length. The optimal solver proves lower bounds: no
solution has fewer moves. Each new bound is printed as soon as it is found, with the time and
the solver that found it. Both solvers stop when the bounds meet; the best solution is then
optimal, proven exactly as either solver proves it alone. With `-t`, they stop at the time
limit, and the output gives the best solution and the bounds; with `-l`, they stop at the first
solution of at most that many moves.

```text
$ omelettehedron solve combined --tier large --optimal-tier large --random 1 --seed 3
    0.000 s  lower 12  optimal
    0.000 s  lower 13  optimal
    0.001 s  lower 14  optimal
    0.002 s  lower 15  optimal
    0.003 s  lower 16  optimal
    0.014 s  upper 23  two-phase  D' U B L' R' U BR' D BR BL' L' U L' B BL L R' D L' D B D L
    0.014 s  lower 17  optimal
    0.084 s  upper 22  two-phase  L U' B' D BL U BR D U BL' L' F R BL U B' U' BR' U' BL BR F
    0.109 s  lower 18  optimal
    0.115 s  upper 21  two-phase  BR BL BR R L U' L U' R D F D' R B L B F BL F' U F'
    1.076 s  upper 20  two-phase  D R' BR BL' B BR' L' U' D B' BR D F' R F' BL U R D R
    1.095 s  lower 19  optimal
    8.207 s  lower 20  two-phase
D R' BR BL' B BR' L' U' D B' BR D F' R F' BL U R D R
20 moves, optimal (solution: two-phase at 1.076 s; lower bound: two-phase at 8.207 s); 8.208 s
```

The two solvers help each other. The optimal solver stops as soon as the two-phase solver's
best length is proven, so it never runs the depth of that length, its longest. With
`--passes`, the two-phase solver's target-length passes start from the largest lower bound
either solver has proven.

* `--tier` and `--optimal-tier` choose the two solvers' tables; without them, each solver takes
  the largest tier whose tables are all there, as `solve two-phase` and `solve optimal` do.
  Both large tiers need about 34 GB (two-phase huge and optimal large about 55 GB); the memory
  is checked before anything is loaded.
* `-j` sets the threads in all; `--two-phase-threads` and `--optimal-threads` set each
  solver's share.
* By default the two-phase solver improves its best solution step by step, as `solve
  two-phase` does: each better solution as soon as it can. `--passes` uses its target-length
  passes instead. Both prove optimality about as fast (below), but the passes find no solution
  until they reach the optimal length: on edge_hard, `passes` found its first solution after
  123 s, the default (`improve`) a 21-move (optimal) one after 0.5 s. Under a time limit,
  `passes` may end with no solution at all.
* `--progress` shows both solvers' progress, each line marked with the solver.

Each solver gets half of the threads, so a combined solve takes about twice as long as the
faster solver alone. But it is never as slow as the slower one, and which one is faster
depends on the position: the corner 6-flip takes 0.63 s combined against 117 s for the
two-phase solver, and a random position 8.5 s against 74 s for the optimal solver. Seconds on
48 threads (combined: 24 + 24), each the solvers' own time; see
[docs/RESULTS.md](docs/RESULTS.md#progress-and-both-solvers-at-once):

| position | optimal | two-phase alone | optimal solver alone | combined (`improve`) | combined (`passes`) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 17-move scramble | 15 | 0.002 | 0.002 | 0.002 | 0.002 |
| 18-move scramble | 18 | 0.109 | 0.521 | 0.274 | 0.222 |
| 19-move scramble | 19 | 1.564 | 2.970 | 1.397 | 1.348 |
| random position (seed 3) | 20 | 4.655 | 73.6 | 8.47 | 9.11 |
| edge_hard | 21 | 62.8 * | 344.7 * | 112.8 | 123.3 |
| corner 6-flip | 20 | 117.2 * | 0.337 | 0.632 | 0.698 |

\* earlier measurements ([docs/RESULTS.md](docs/RESULTS.md)), not rerun.


## WebAssembly demo

`wasm/` holds a browser demo: the solver as a WebAssembly module, a JavaScript wrapper and a
static page. It offers the tiny, small and medium tiers (the page calls them Light, Standard and
Faster; the large tier needs 8 GB, and wasm32 has a 4 GB address space) and face turns only (no
`--moves`).

```sh
# (the Rust version of the GitHub Pages site, wasm/rust-version: build-wasm.sh uses it)
rustup toolchain install "$(cat wasm/rust-version)" --profile minimal \
    --target wasm32-wasip1 --target wasm32-wasip1-threads
scripts/build-wasm.sh        # wasm/dist/: omelettehedron.wasm, omelettehedron-threads.wasm,
                             # omelettehedron-threads-medium.wasm
bun wasm/serve.mjs           # then open http://localhost:8000/
bun wasm/test.mjs            # headless test in Bun (FTO_TABLES=DIR also compares the tables and
                             # loads the medium tier; FTO_TEST_MEDIUM_GENERATE=1 generates it)
bun wasm/bench.mjs [--tier tiny|small|medium] [--threads N] [--tables DIR] [--count 10] [--seconds 5]
```

The demo is deployed to GitHub Pages (https://omelettehedron.arhan.sh/) by
`.github/workflows/pages.yml` on every push to the default branch: `scripts/build-pages.sh DIR`
builds the three modules and copies only the public files. Pages cannot send the cross-origin
isolation headers, so the page registers `wasm/coi-sw.js`, a service worker that adds them, and
reloads once; where a browser refuses, the demo runs on one thread.

* `omelettehedron.wasm` (`wasm32-wasip1`) runs on one thread anywhere, with any of the tiers; its
  memory grows as needed. `omelettehedron-threads.wasm` (`wasm32-wasip1-threads`) runs
  rayon's thread pool on wasi-threads. It needs `SharedArrayBuffer`, so a browser page must be
  cross-origin isolated (the COOP/COEP headers that `wasm/serve.mjs` sends; GitHub Pages
  cannot send them). Its memory is fixed from the start: growing the shared memory while
  threads run is unsafe in V8 (see `wasm/build.rs`). It has 1 GiB, room for the tiny and small
  tiers;
  `omelettehedron-threads-medium.wasm`, the same module built with 2 GiB (feature `medium`),
  has room for the medium tier. Asked to generate tables that do not fit, a module says so
  instead of trapping.
* The medium tier's A-centre/edge index has 4,127,692,800 entries, just below 2^32: every
  index is a sum of products no larger than itself, so the 32-bit `usize` of wasm32 computes
  the same indices (checked on the host with checked 32-bit arithmetic, by a fingerprint
  test that `scripts/check.sh` also runs on wasm32, and by the demo test's node counts). The
  CLI refuses the large and huge tiers on 32-bit targets.
* A table file is copied into the module in 16 MiB pieces and used where it lies (its header
  is removed in place), so loading the medium tier needs no second copy of its 1.03 GB.
* `wasm/omelettehedron.mjs` provides the WASI functions the module imports and a worker pool
  for its threads. `FtoSolver` generates or loads the tables, solves scrambles optimally or
  with a time limit, solves patterns, makes random-state scrambles and checks solutions on the
  sticker model.
  A solve can be cancelled from another thread (`cancel`), which moves the module's clock past
  its time limit; this needs shared memory.
* The page (`wasm/index.html`, run in a worker by `wasm/demo-worker.mjs`) starts with every core
  when it is cross-origin isolated, else with one thread. The visitor chooses the tables:
  Light (tiny, 42 MB), Standard (small, 134 MB) or Faster (medium, 1.04 GB); the choice is
  remembered. Phones and tablets start with Light (Standard took too long to prepare on an
  iPhone), other devices with Standard. The page generates the chosen tables, warms up the
  engine and keeps the tables in IndexedDB (one entry per tier) for later visits. If a tier
  cannot be prepared (e.g. not enough memory), it goes back to the next smaller one and says
  so. The page shows each better solution while solving, and the proven lower bound
  ("shortest possible: at least N moves"). Three modes: best solution in a given time;
  **keep improving** (shows better and better solutions, no time limit, until the last is
  proven shortest; stop any time); and **shortest only** (shows just the final answer, but
  its proof is about 2.6x faster on one thread). Run to the end, the last two give the
  same answer; the labels say so because users took them for the same thing. **Stop** cancels a solve. Without shared memory, it restarts the worker and reloads
  the stored tables.
* **Invert** turns the moves in the box around (reversed, each turned the other way), so a
  solution found elsewhere becomes a scramble of the position it solves; solve that to find
  other solutions. It works on the text alone, before the tables are ready too.
* The page also solves a pattern (see [Patterns](#patterns)): the visitor colours a picture of
  the front and the back (`wasm/pattern-picture.mjs`, `wasm/pattern-input.mjs`; the palette is
  their colour scheme, kept in localStorage), and gets a solution and a scramble that makes the
  pattern, with its start hold. Mistakes are explained and the stickers concerned outlined.
* Tables can also be loaded from the native `gen two-phase --tier tiny`, `small` or `medium` files:
  the formats are the same, and the module generates byte-identical files.

Measured in Bun 1.4.4 (JavaScriptCore, Safari's engine) on the 48-core EPYC VM (small tier;
generation and search re-measured on 2026-11-11 after `opt/wasm-gen`, quiet machine):

Until `opt/wasm-gen`, threads made generation slower, not faster (6 threads 48.6 s against 30.9 s
on one): the generator read `OnceLock` statics per entry, and in WebAssembly each such read is an
atomic load, which in JavaScriptCore (Bun, Safari, every browser on iOS) makes the threads
contend; the generator's graphs and indices now hold those tables.

module grows to 352 MiB while generating and 314 MiB while loading table files (358 MiB before
the edge symmetry tables were made in place, 428 MiB before the files were loaded in place). The optimal solves of short scrambles (6-14 moves) take
milliseconds, with the same node counts as natively on one thread.

A medium-tier node costs about twice as much in WebAssembly as a small-tier one (natively they
cost the same, probably because the native build puts the tables on huge pages), so in the
browser proofs take about a fifth of the time with the medium tier rather than a tenth. The optimal solves of the
test scrambles have the native one-thread node counts with either tier.

Threads helped little in JavaScriptCore. Its WebAssembly atomic loads behave like locked
read-modify-writes. In a micro-benchmark, eight threads reading shared words got 7 M loads/s
each, against 226 M/s for one thread. The search read atomics for every node: the `OnceLock`
statics such as `fast()`, and the stop and best-length flags. Since the search keeps its tables
in plain fields and caches the flags per worker (`opt/cbref`, `opt/atomics`, `opt/stopcache`),
8 threads search 144 M nodes/s instead of 34.7 M (6 random positions, 5 s each; one thread
20 M); the table generator still reads `OnceLock`s often. V8 (Chrome) and
SpiderMonkey (Firefox) compile atomic loads on x86 to plain loads, so they should scale like
the native build. No browser was available to measure this. Before a solve, the page runs a
short warm-up so that V8 compiles the search with its optimizing tier.

The JavaScript layer provides the WASI functions, the thread pool and the reactor initializer.
Three rules keep it working in browsers: initial memory equals maximum memory, threads never
exit, and a warm-up solve runs for V8.

## Tests

```sh
cargo test --release
cargo test --release -- --ignored   # the slow ones: 400-position brute force, stress tests
scripts/check.sh      # fmt, clippy (pedantic, -D warnings), tests, no runtime asserts
```

`FTO_CHECK_FAST=1 scripts/check.sh` (host tests, clippy, fmt; minutes) is the check for routine
changes, plus the WebAssembly demo test (`scripts/build-wasm.sh`, `bun wasm/test.mjs`) when a
change touches the demo, since every push to `main` redeploys it. The full `scripts/check.sh`
adds the backend matrix (the scalar fallback, aarch64 under qemu, wasm32 under bun's WASI and
the demo module; about an hour) and runs before each release, a version tag on a commit that
passed it (v0.1.0 is the first).

Non-test code does not panic: `unwrap`, `expect`, `panic!` and friends are denied by clippy,
and `scripts/check.sh` rejects runtime `assert!` outside `#[cfg(test)]` code.

The tests cover:
* the geometry, against values from an independent sticker model checked with GAP;
* notation and frame handling, via physical sticker simulation;
* coordinates and symmetry reduction;
* pruning-table consistency;
* optimal mode, against a meet-in-the-middle brute force, with and without slice turns;
* the move-order rules: per axis against the axis group, and the canonical sequences of each
  length up to 5 (4 with slices) against breadth-first search (every position reached; exactly
  once up to length 4);
* search coverage: with every pruning table zeroed, each allowed phase-1 move sequence is
  reached exactly once (checked against exact counts, at 1 and 8 threads, with and without
  slices);
* phase-2 membership, including the centres;
* every stopping rule;
* randomised stress (ignored by default): every move set with random stopping rules and thread
  counts, solutions checked on the sticker model, move families, improvement events, and
  optimal lengths independent of the thread count.
* the optimal solver (`tests/optimal.rs`): optimal lengths against the brute force and against
  the two-phase solver's optimal mode, with and without slice turns, `-n` against brute-force
  enumeration of all optimal solutions, the other stopping rules, deterministic node counts and
  table consistency.
* the command line (`tests/cli.rs`): the same output from the three `solve` methods (with
  `-q`, `--batch` and positions on stdin), solutions printed at once without a limit,
  patterns on stdin (one with a mistake) and as arguments, the tier chosen from the table files
  (and nothing generated without `--tier`), and `show` and `symmetric` without tables.
